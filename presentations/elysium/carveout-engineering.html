The carve-out, in engineering detail — and what the system does that a carve-out cannot

Twenty-six repositories. Six of them contribute code to a self-run hand-over; twenty do not. This document walks the boundary repository by repository, states plainly what a reduced package can and cannot do, and then does the more useful thing — prices the next two years of the mandate both ways, because that is what actually decides it.

01

What the contract specifies, and what the platform is

Both of the following are true, and the distance between them is the entire subject of this document. The engagement specifies a delta-neutral basis strategy on BTC, ETH and SOL across major venues. What was built to deliver it is a twenty-six repository platform.

The deliverable is narrowTwo archetypes, four venues, one collateral protocol. That is what was commissioned and it is what a carve-out contains
The platform is notSix repositories contribute to the hand-over; twenty exist to make the strategy defensible, evolvable and operable
The gap is not code volumeIt is the difference between a program that trades and a system that can be trusted, tuned and extended — see §8 for what that costs as the mandate grows
Why the distinction matters more than it sounds

A reasonable CTO reading the contract would conclude that the deliverable is a trading program. That reading is correct. If the question is "does the strategy run and make money", a reduced package answers it: two engines, four venue adapters, the staking leg, a runner, and the strategy trades.

The reason twenty other repositories exist is that "does it run" is not the question a regulated allocator ends up asking. The questions that actually arrive are these, and each one is answered by a different part of the platform rather than by the strategy code:

  • Is the backtest that justified this allocation reproducible, and provably the same program as the live one?
  • When the basis compresses, who re-parameterises, on what evidence, and how do we know the new parameters are better rather than merely more recent?
  • When a venue withdraws collateral acceptance mid-position, what happens in the next sixty seconds?
  • Which of last month's return was strategy and which was execution, per venue, per leg?
  • Where did this number in the investor report come from, and can it be rebuilt from source?
  • When we add a second strategy, or a fifth venue, or a second share class, what does that cost?

None of those are answered by the code that places the trade. That is the honest shape of the trade-off, and the rest of this document quantifies it repository by repository rather than asserting it.

A note on what this document is not

It is not an argument that a carve-out cannot work. It can, we built the hand-over package for it, and for an organisation that wants to own its operational stack it may be the right answer. It is an attempt to make the decision legible — because the parts that are easy to underestimate are precisely the parts that took us longest, and we would rather you priced them before electing than discovered them afterwards.

03

Inside the six: what reduction actually removes

"Reduced" does most of the work in this document, so it is worth being concrete about it. In each of the six, the part that ships is small and the part that stays is the part that made the small part cheap to write.

2 of 8 archetypesThe carry package alone holds eight; you receive two, without the family machinery around them
4 of 13 venuesAdapters for your venues only — and the registry that makes venues interchangeable stays
1 feature calculatorStaking yield. The funding side is produced by a pipeline you would not have
Repository by repository — the closure, and what sits immediately outside it

strategy-service — two modules out of a family, out of a platform

The carry-and-yield package alone contains eight archetype modules; you receive two of them. Sitting alongside them in the same package are the recursive-staked variant, lending rotation, passive staking, dated basis, and the funding-dispersion analysis that decides which coins are worth holding at all. Underneath, the repository's other sub-packages own risk, position monitoring and P&L attribution — which is why the two modules you receive are as short as they are. They delegate.

The consequence for a receiving engineer: the two modules are readable and self-contained at the decision level, and they assume a caller that supplies features, a ledger that accepts writes, a risk layer that vetoes, and a position monitor that reconciles. Those assumptions are interfaces in the package you get and implementations in packages you do not.

execution-service — connectivity out of an execution platform

Four venue adapters out of thirteen, and the custody pair. What stays is the layer that decides how an order is worked: the algorithm library, the routing, the policy registry, and the paper matching engine that makes a backtest fill model match the live one. Also staying: the adapters for every other venue, which is what makes adding a fifth venue an integration rather than a project.

unified-api-contracts and unified-trading-library — the closure, not the library

These two are where "reduced" is least intuitive. Both are large, both are imported by everything, and the subset you need is defined by what your engines actually reach — computable exactly, because the tier rule guarantees imports only point downward. What arrives is that closure: your venue and collateral registries, the instruction and ledger models, config and cloud interfaces, manifest and ledger writers, the on-chain rate primitives.

What stays inside the same repositories is instructive, because it is the machinery this document keeps referring to: the allocation package, chaos injection, circuit breakers, client lifecycle, the streaming event facade, the domain client with its path registry and typed readers. Those are not withheld to be difficult — they are the multi-tenant, multi-strategy platform, and a single-strategy package does not import them.

features-service and instruments-service — one calculator and one registry

You receive the staking-yield aggregator and a minimal venue-identifier registry. What stays is the reason those two are trustworthy: formula versioning that makes a feature reproducible at the version a backtest used, and the reference-data lifecycle that knows when an instrument listed, when it delisted, and what its coverage window is. A minimal registry tells you a venue's identifier. It does not tell you that the venue changed its contract specification last March.

"Light" adapters — connectivity without the estate

You receive working exchange connectivity: authenticate, place, cancel, read fills, sign through custody. That is genuinely what an adapter is. What surrounds an adapter in this platform is a different matter, and it is where most of the operational value sits.

You getDirect connectivity to your four venues plus the two custody providers — authenticate, place, cancel, read
You do not getAutomated position and balance reconciliation against venue truth
NorCustody-provider abstraction, the treasury-and-trading split, per-client separation, or share-class dynamics
The five things that sit around an adapter, and what each one is worth
Adapter, and adapter-in-a-platform The column on the right is what does not transfer
ConcernWhat the light adapter doesWhat the platform does around it
Reconciliation Reports the fills the venue returned Continuously reconciles believed position and balance against venue truth, detects drift, and treats a mismatch as an incident with a defined response rather than a logging line
Custody abstraction Signs through the provider it was written for Treats the signing surface as pluggable, so moving from one custodian to another — or running a tri-party mirror alongside direct custody — is a configuration change and a credential handover, not an execution-path rewrite
Treasury and trading split Trades whatever is in the account it points at Separates a per-share-class treasury from per-strategy trading wallets, moves capital between them on a reserve ratio, and bridges across chains as part of the same flow — so idle capital is not sitting in a trading wallet and a strategy cannot consume more than its allocation
Per-client separation One account, one book One isolated process per client with a data model that refuses to represent a cross-client transfer, so isolation is structural rather than procedural — the property an allocator's operational due diligence asks about
Share-class dynamics Not modelled Subscriptions and redemptions land against a share class, size positions accordingly, and flow through to fee and high-water-mark accounting at the class level

The pattern across all five: the adapter answers "can I place an order at this venue". The platform answers "is what I believe about this venue true, whose money is it, and what happens when the answer changes". A single strategy at a single client with one custodian can live without the second question for a while. A fund administrator asking for a monthly reconciliation cannot.

05

Static config versus a system that keeps looking

This is the single largest difference and the easiest to underestimate, because it does not look like missing code. A carve-out receives one parameter set, optimised as at the hand-over date. What stays behind is the machinery that found it and the machinery that keeps re-finding it.

You receiveOne frozen, optimised configuration — correct for the regime it was fitted in
You do not receiveParameter search, regime detection, or the dynamism that reallocates as conditions move
Nor the governanceVersion control over which config is live, the promotion ladder that let it become live, and the compliance trail behind both
What "dynamic" means concretely — six mechanisms, and what a static config does instead
Dynamism, itemised Each row is a running mechanism, not a roadmap item
MechanismWhat it does in the platformStatic-config equivalent
Dynamic coin rotation Ranks the coin universe continuously on current funding across venues, with smoothing to damp turnover, and rotates the book toward where the carry actually is A fixed coin list. When funding on one name compresses, the position stays
Dynamic venue and structure switching Moves between staked-basis and cash-basis per venue as collateral acceptance and funding change, respecting each venue's rules A fixed structure per venue, chosen once
Portfolio allocation Allocates across strategies, venues and versions, with capital moving to the better risk-adjusted opportunity One strategy, one allocation, adjusted manually
Circuit breakers Feed health, staleness and error classification drive automatic breakers that stop new signals and then halt, with resume gated by a defined recovery matrix Whatever the runner does on an exception
Version governance A strategy climbs candidate, one-day paper, early live, full live on one config surface, with pre-flight gates at each step and an operator approving every fill for the first three trading days Deploy and hope. The tested artefact and the promoted artefact are separate things
Compliance trail Every instruction carries its archetype build, slot version, config hash and attestations; every event carries the same tag; any run replays deterministically from its manifest Logs. Reconstructing why a decision was made in October is an investigation
The compounding point

A frozen optimum decays. Not because the parameters were wrong, but because the regime they were fitted in moves — and in today's compressed basis environment it moves quickly. Dynamic switching between structures and venues is, on our own measurement, one of the larger contributors to maintaining returns rather than a refinement on top of them. A static configuration is therefore not a slightly worse version of the same strategy; it is a strategy with a different and shorter half-life.

06

The economics of shared infrastructure

A managed platform is cheaper than a dedicated one for a structural reason rather than a commercial one: the expensive components are fixed-cost and shared, so their cost per strategy falls as the platform carries more strategies. A carve-out converts all of it into your fixed cost, at a scale of one.

Shared, not duplicatedCapture, storage, reference data, CI, monitoring and the escalation fleet serve every strategy at once
Your cost is marginalYou pay for your slice of a running estate, not for standing one up
A carve-out inverts itEvery shared component becomes a dedicated component, at single-strategy scale
Which costs are shared, which are yours alone, and what changes under a carve-out

ComponentUnder the managed platformUnder a carve-out
Market-data capture and storageOne estate serving every strategy and client; your slice is marginal storage and computeYou capture only what you need, and lose the history behind everything you would want to evidence
Data vendor licencesHeld centrally and amortised across the estateProcured by you, priced for one consumer
Reference data and venue researchMaintained once — collateral matrices live-probed, contract changes tracked — and consumed by everythingA snapshot, maintained by you, re-probed by you when a venue changes its risk engine
CI, quality gates, releaseOne pipeline, one gate, every repositoryYours to build and run for one repository
Monitoring, alerting, on-callOne rotation and one alerting estate across all strategiesA rotation for one strategy, at the same fixed human cost
Autonomous escalationShared fleet handling restarts, resource failures and pipeline recovery before a human is involvedNot available. The floor is a human on a pager
ComputeSpot-first with preemption recovery, admission sized to measured load, and rightsizing audits — across a fleet where the savings compoundYours to provision, and the same savings require the same engineering

The asymmetry is worth stating directly: the components that dominate the cost of running a trading platform are the ones least sensitive to how many strategies run on it. A monitoring rotation costs the same for one strategy as for eight. A capture estate costs marginally more for the ninth venue than the eighth. A release pipeline is a fixed build. That is what makes a shared platform economically different in kind rather than in degree — and it is why a carve-out's true cost is rarely the hand-over fee.

There is a second-order effect worth naming for a CTO specifically. Because every strategy on the platform shares the instruction contract, the ledger and the reconciliation spine, a capability built for one becomes available to all of them — and that includes capabilities built for other clients. Execution improvements, a new venue integration, a better fill model, tighter reconciliation: these arrive as platform upgrades rather than as per-client projects. A carve-out steps out of that flow permanently, because the snapshot is frozen at hand-over by design.

TradFi as the worked example of a discontinuity

We single this one out because it is the direction where the difference between the two paths stops being a matter of degree. A regulated futures venue is not another venue with a different symbol format. It brings a different protocol family rather than REST and WebSocket, a clearing relationship that has to be established and maintained, regulatory reporting obligations, a margin model computed by the clearing house rather than by a collateral haircut, and cash settlement on a settlement cycle rather than a wallet transfer. Each of those touches a different layer of a trading system.

On the platform, a meaningful part of that groundwork already exists and is not something you would be funding from zero: a broker-gateway service that manages the long-lived connection to a regulated broker, a TradFi market-data path with its own vendor integration and instrument definitions for futures and options, an options pricing service that already computes greeks and carry-family rates for marked positions, and a delta-one feature family. Adding a regulated venue is therefore an integration project with a compliance workstream beside it.

Standalone, after a carve-out, the same ambition means building a futures-capable trading system next to your crypto one — new connectivity, new instrument model, new margin model, new settlement path, new reporting — and then reconciling the two. That is the point at which the carve-out decision stops being about a retainer and starts being about a multi-year engineering commitment.

The compounding effect, and why it only runs one way

Because every strategy on the platform shares the instruction contract, the ledger and the reconciliation spine, a capability built for one becomes available to all of them — including capabilities built for other clients and for our own book. A better fill model, a tighter reconciliation, a new venue integration, an improvement to how collateral is monitored: these arrive as platform upgrades rather than as per-client projects.

A carve-out steps out of that flow permanently and by design, because the licensed snapshot is frozen at hand-over. That is not a penalty clause — it is the only honest way to licence components we continue to develop. But it does mean the gap between what you hold and what the platform does widens every month, starting on day one.

The asymmetry worth naming

There is a version of this decision that has nothing to do with cost. A managed service you later conclude you did not need is a recoverable mistake — you elect the carve-out, the package is there, you take it. A self-run estate that turns out to be under-built is discovered with client capital deployed, and it is not recoverable on the same timescale. The downside is asymmetric in a way that the monthly numbers do not capture, and in our experience that is the consideration that actually decides it.

09

An honest summary of the trade

A carve-out gives you a working implementation of exactly what was specified, and full ownership of it. That is a real thing and we are not going to argue you out of wanting it. But it is the wrong question asked in isolation — the answer depends almost entirely on where the mandate is going, not on where it is.

What transfers wellThe strategy logic, the venue connectivity, and the decision-level code — all of it readable and self-contained
What transfers poorlyAnything whose value comes from being maintained: reference data, calibration, coverage accounting, operational rehearsal
What does not transferThe history behind the numbers, the proof that live matches backtest, and the flow of improvements over time
Decide against a roadmapA static mandate makes the carve-out defensible; any of the growth directions in §8 make it the more expensive path by a widening margin
The three questions we would ask in your position

First — what am I actually buying if I stay? Not access to twenty repositories as a catalogue item. Three specific things: the evidence layer that makes a return defensible to an investor and an auditor; the dynamism that keeps a carry book earning as regimes move; and a marginal cost of expansion low enough that adding a venue or a second strategy is a conversation rather than a project.

Second — what is the real cost of leaving? Not the hand-over fee. It is the operational estate: a release process, a monitoring rotation, reconciliation, reporting, and the engineering to keep an adapter working when a venue changes something. And it is the standing loss of the improvement flow, since the licensed snapshot is frozen at hand-over.

Third — where is the genuine risk in staying? Concentration on a single provider, and we would rather answer it than have it sit unspoken. The mitigations are structural rather than promissory: the code is yours under the agreement regardless, the carve-out package exists and is documented, the strategy logic is readable rather than obfuscated, the platform runs on two clouds from one codebase, and the venue and custody relationships are held by you rather than by us. If you elect to leave, you leave with the thing you commissioned and a documented route to running it. That option not expiring is, we think, the right basis for a long relationship.

What we would suggest

Two things, in order. First, decide it against your own twenty-four month roadmap rather than against today's mandate. If the honest answer is that this stays BTC, ETH and SOL, on four venues, in one structure, for one share class, indefinitely — then the carve-out is a defensible choice, the package exists, and we will support the hand-over properly. If any of more coins, a fifth venue, a second staking protocol, a second archetype, a second share class or a regulated venue is plausible in that window, then §8 is the section that decides this, not §2. Tell us the roadmap and we will price it both ways, including the parts that are inconvenient for us.

Second, run the managed service through the first regime change before electing anything. The claim in §5 — that a frozen optimum decays — is the one assertion in this document you can test cheaply and directly, by watching what the dynamic layer does when the basis moves against the parameters it was fitted on. If it earns its place, the question answers itself. If it does not, you will have a far better-informed view of what you would be taking on, and the package will still be there. Nothing about waiting costs you optionality.

And to be direct about our own position

We would rather you stayed, and the reason is not only commercial. A carve-out that under-delivers reflects on the strategy we designed, whoever is operating it at the time — and we would be watching that happen without the ability to fix it. If you do elect it, we would rather do the hand-over thoroughly, with the gaps named in advance and the manifest corrected, than have it go out fast and half-documented. That is the same interest either way: this working is worth more to us than the difference between the two fee structures.
