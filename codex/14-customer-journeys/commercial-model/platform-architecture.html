Hard architectural rule

Funds never move between clients. Every transfer is scoped to a single client, and the constraint is enforced in the data model itself: a ledger row that would cross a client boundary raises at construction time rather than being caught by review. Each client also runs in its own isolated process. "Cross-client rebalancing" is not a feature we have; it is a condition the code refuses to represent.

CARRY_STAKED_BASIS: the structure, and the two we deleted

The trade deploys USDC into the native asset, stakes it into the liquid staking token, posts that LST at the perp venue as cross-margin, and shorts the equivalent perp. Delta is zero because the short cancels the long exposure the LST carries. You earn the staking yield on the principal and the funding on the short, from the same capital, which is the whole point, and also the reason the structure is fussy about venues.

1. SWAP USDC → ETH / SOL on a spot venue 2. STAKE ETH → stETH / wstETH via Lido on Ethereum mainnet 3. TRANSFER LST → perp venue posted as cross-margin 4. SHORT perp sized to net delta ≈ 0 net carry (annualised, bps) = staking_apy_total + funding_apy − fees staking_apy_total = on-chain rate growth + restaking rewards + seasonal LST rewards − dust slippage

One structure is allowed: the LST is the margin. That narrowing was deliberate: we built the two obvious alternatives, measured them, and deleted both rather than leave them behind a config flag. Split-stake (stake half off-venue, post the rest as cash margin) is strictly dominated by plain basis at twice the size whenever funding > staking / 3, which is the prevailing regime for BTC, ETH and SOL; in the narrow regime where it could win, the leveraged variant beats it anyway. Borrow-against-collateral (pledge the LST, borrow USDC, post that as margin) dies on the stablecoin borrow rate, typically 5–8% annualised, which erodes the basis faster than staking plus funding earns it back. Neither has a regime where it is the right answer, so neither survived.

Where the venue won't take staked collateral, we skip the staking

This is the part worth understanding properly, because it is what makes the strategy portable across a venue set rather than tied to one venue's rules. Eligibility is not configured per slot and not asserted in a document: it is derived at run time from a collateral registry in the code, which holds a row per (venue, token) with an accepted flag, a live-probed haircut, the margin mode it applies under, and a citation. The engine asks that registry whether this venue accepts this LST as cross-margin.

If the answer is no, the staked-basis slot is simply never generated for that venue, and the capital runs CARRY_BASIS_PERP there instead, capturing funding on cash margin. We skip the staking leg rather than forcing it through a worse structure. That is the same conclusion the split-stake analysis above reached, expressed as behaviour: a venue that won't take your LST is a venue where the staking leg does not pay for itself, so the platform takes the funding and leaves the staking.

Binance is the live example in your own venue set: its cross-collateral feature was retired and it now accepts only a defined list of majors and stablecoins as futures collateral, so no staked-basis slot exists there and the capital runs plain basis. That is a structural fact about Binance, not a gap in the build.

The corollary is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to overstate in both directions. Adding a venue or an LST later does not require touching the engine or the catalogue: the registry row is the whole of the wiring, and slots regenerate from it automatically. But the row is the last step, not the work. Behind it sits a live haircut probe against the venue's own risk engine, the wrap-step decision, an on-chain rate reader for the new token, rebasing reconciliation, kill-switch and haircut calibration, a runbook and a cutover. Fifty to a hundred hours, typically. What the platform bought is that this is now weeks of bounded integration rather than a quarter of rebuilding , which is exactly the difference the Phase 2 investment paid for.

Rebasing versus wrapped: the leg shape changes per venue

A detail that sounds pedantic and is not, because getting it wrong marks the position wrong every single day. Lido's stETH is rebasing: your balance grows and the price stays near parity. Its wrapped form, wstETH, is the opposite: balance held constant, price accretes. Venues are built for one or the other, and the staking leg is assembled differently depending on which the venue expects.

Neither would show up as an error. Both would show up as unexplained P&L drift months later.

The yield is measured on-chain, not taken from a vendor

Staking APY is reconstructed from the LST contract's own exchange rate: the growth in what one token is worth in the underlying asset, annualised:

staking_apy_bps = (( rate[t] / rate[t−1] ) ^ 365 − 1) × 10,000 rate[t] read directly from the issuer contract stETH → getPooledEthByShares() wstETH → stEthPerToken()

That is what the position would actually have earned, rather than a modelled or self-reported figure. We do not take LST yields from data aggregators; those are useful for protocol context and total-value-locked, and are not the yield source here. Where an issuer publishes its own rate we audit against it; the last such check came back at zero basis points of drift. Funding APY is equally unmodelled: the venue's published rate multiplied by its settlement cycles per day and annualised, straight arithmetic on raw venue data. And where a component is deliberately not modelled (idle yield on USDC margin at venues that pay it), and it contributes zero rather than an estimate, so the number you see is a floor rather than a hope.

Holding the hedge as the peg moves

Hedge sizing is recomputed every tick rather than set once at entry, from the LST's current on-chain exchange rate and the venue's margin haircut, so as the LST accretes against the underlying, the short grows with it. A hysteresis band of roughly 25 basis points on peg drift, about three standard deviations of daily movement, governs when a rebalance actually fires, so the position does not churn against noise. If the rate reading goes stale beyond five minutes the engine falls back to a one-to-one rate and logs it rather than trading on a stale number: the conservative direction, since it under-hedges nothing and simply stops chasing.

CARRY_BASIS_PERP: funding capture on cash margin

The same machinery with the staking leg removed: spot long against a short perp, margined in USDC or USDT, capturing the funding rate alone. It is what runs wherever the staked structure is ineligible, and it is a first-class archetype rather than a degraded mode; most of the venue surface in your set runs it.

On how quickly things can be added

This document says several times that new venues, LSTs and archetypes are cheap to add, and we would rather you read that precisely than generously. It does not mean the work is trivial: each addition carries live venue probing, calibration, reconciliation, validation and a cutover, and we price it accordingly. It means the marginal cost is now bounded and predictable, because the expensive part was paid once: the registries, the leg builder, the determinism spine, the ledger, the reconciliation and the deployment path all already exist and do not get rebuilt per venue. That is the return on the Phase 2 investment, not that expansion is free, but that the second venue costs a fraction of what the first one did, and the tenth costs no more than the second.

DeFi and on-chain: the staking leg

The staked side reads on-chain rather than from a vendor, which is deliberate: the staking yield in a backtest and the staking yield in a live run come from the same contract calls. For Lido that means reading the pooled-ETH and per-token exchange-rate functions directly. Around that, the platform captures LST exchange rates and staking yields, oracle prices, lending indices and risk parameters across the major lending protocols, DEX swaps and pool state, liquidation and flash-loan events, vault share prices, restaking rewards and slashing events, and gas. Your two archetypes consume a small part of that: LST rates, staking yields, oracle prices, Ethereum gas, and the rest is what makes the adjacent Carry & Yield archetypes in §3 cheap to stand up.

Live and paper do not need history

One detail that affects how quickly a new venue can go live: the live ranking signal reads the current funding snapshot, not a funding history. Each of your four venues exposes it publicly and without authentication (Binance and Bybit in one call across all symbols, OKX and Deribit per instrument) and the platform normalises settlement cadence per venue rather than assuming eight hours. So a venue with no historical backfill is not blocked from paper or live; it is warned about and proceeds. A backtest, of course, still needs the history.

The unglamorous foundation

We treat missing data as four distinct states, not one: the venue genuinely had nothing, we never requested it, we requested it and retrieval failed, or it is not applicable to that instrument. Collapsing those into a single empty cell is how survivorship bias enters a backtest silently: you quietly drop the periods that were hardest to capture, which are usually the periods that mattered. Every shard carries its state explicitly, and the coverage denominator is computed from the capture universe rather than from what happened to arrive.

For your specific slice (spot, perpetual, funding and staking history on BTC, ETH and SOL across your four venues, plus the on-chain staking and Ethereum gas series) the fully backfilled volume is a few terabytes and several billion records. As of the July programme review that backfill was roughly half complete, and it is the first item on the critical path in §12.

Batch, paper, live: one program

A backtest is only evidence if the live system is the same program. On this platform it literally is: batch, paper and live enter the same decision function and diverge only where an order meets a market.

The determinism spine, the decomposition, and how each mode reads and writes

The invariant is strict. If we paper-trade a window and then backtest the same window, every trade in the backtest must also appear in the paper run: same instructions, same sizes, same sequence. That makes the paper-versus-batch comparison a determinism proof rather than a tolerance check: a non-zero difference is a bug in one of three specific places: non-determinism in the decision path, a gap in input capture, or drift between the two fill models, and never "within threshold".

The decomposition is what makes this useful rather than merely tidy:

live − batch = (paper − batch) + (live − paper) └ determinism ┘ └ execution alpha ┘ must be zero the number we or it's a bug actually want to measure

How each mode reads and writes

The same services run in both modes; what changes is the transport, and that choice is made by deployment configuration rather than by the service. In batch mode object storage is both the message bus and the persistence layer: the producer's write is the consumer's read, which is what makes a batch run reproducible from nothing but its manifest. In live mode the inner cascade uses ordered streams because it needs per-shard ordering and replay from a checkpoint when a consumer restarts, while broadcast to unrelated consumers uses a publish-subscribe topic. When strategy and execution are co-located, the transport collapses to in-memory and the hop disappears entirely.

Execution: how the algorithm is chosen

Algorithm resolution, the ownership boundary, and the instruction contract

A strategy emits an instruction describing intent, not mechanics: the legs, the target state, and the constraints. Multi-leg instructions carry an execution mode, which is where the interesting behaviour lives. Fully atomic execution requires all legs or none. Leader-and-hedge places the leader leg first and only sends the hedge if the leader is accepted, inside a hedge deadline, and if the hedge fails or times out it applies a compensation policy to unwind the leader rather than leaving a naked position. If it cannot unwind cleanly it reports a naked position and alerts, and never a false clean result. Sequenced-with-pacing works the legs over time under a delta-neutrality constraint held through execution, not merely at the end of it.

Given that intent, a policy registry resolves which algorithm actually runs. Rules match on the shape of the order and the context around it, and the resolved algorithm is recorded so the choice is auditable after the fact. This is the mechanism behind "dynamic" execution: nothing in your strategy configuration names a schedule, and changing how orders are worked does not require touching the strategy.

Querying the decision-making, remotely

A managed service that you cannot interrogate is a black box, and a black box is not something a regulated allocator should be comfortable with. So under Option A the strategy's reasoning is queryable remotely and read-only, without anything being shipped to you: the instruction tape as it is written, the inputs each decision was taken on: the funding snapshot per venue, the on-chain LST rate, the haircut in force, the resolved execution policy, and the attribution of the resulting P&L by factor, layer, venue and instrument. Every instruction carries its own attestations, so "why did it do that, on 14 October, on OKX" is a query rather than an investigation.

Holding the source tells you what the strategy would do. The tape, its inputs and its attribution tell you what it did, and why, which is the thing you actually have to explain to an investor.

The contract is published below because you should be able to see how it works before deciding whether it is useful. Five rules govern it; two of them are why the arrangement holds.

  • Target state, not deltas. A strategy emits the desired end state, never the change from current: target_position_units = 10, not buy 5 more. Execution reconciles to it. This is what makes instructions idempotent: emitting the same target twice is a no-op, and a restart, a partial fill or a reconciliation drift cannot leave the two sides disagreeing about what "current" was.
  • Intent and constraints, not algorithm prescriptions. The strategy says what it wants and by when; execution decides how. A strategy that named its own schedule would be pinning execution quality to whatever was true when it was written, and would also be the wrong place for that decision to live.

Where the contract is headed: reference price, reference position, credit

The envelope already carries a reference_price on every instruction: the market level the strategy believed was true the moment it was emitted, together with a mark mode telling execution whether to hold that price constant as sent or keep marking it against the underlying while the order is worked. It exists because a price is allowed to move between instruction and fill; that is what the reference price accounts for, not a fault condition.

A position can go stale in a different way than a price can: a strategy and execution simply disagreeing about what is actually held is not a market move, it is the two sides losing sync. The next layer on the roadmap adds a reference_position to the same envelope: the strategy's believed position, per venue it is eligible to trade on, checked against what execution actually sees the instant an instruction lands. A mismatch is treated as an integrity failure, using the same block, scale-down or monitor consequence the risk gate already applies to any rule it fires, not a new decision path.

Travelling alongside it is credit: a tolerance band or a fire-or-don't threshold that the strategy computes and owns, with execution only consuming whatever is cached to decide whether and how aggressively to act on it. None of this reaches into how an order is actually worked: it sets the boundary the algorithm library described above operates inside, the same relationship execution_policy_ref already has with the algorithm it resolves to: strategy states the governing limits, execution's own logic decides the mechanics within them.

The choices behind that picture

Four of them are worth stating explicitly, because each was a decision with a live alternative we rejected.

  • Storage: partitioned Parquet, queried in place. Everything lands as Parquet under a day= partition with the dimensions that matter for that dataset below it. Analytics reads it through external tables that point at the files rather than importing them, and the partition filter is required: a query that forgets to constrain the date is rejected rather than quietly scanning the estate. The alternative, loading everything into a warehouse, buys faster scans at the cost of a second copy that has to be kept honest. We would rather have one system of record.
  • Messaging: ordered where it matters, multicast where it doesn't. The live inner cascade uses ordered streams with consumer groups, because a restarted consumer must resume exactly where it stopped and candles must be built in sequence. Cross-service events go out on a publish–subscribe topic instead: strategy, alerting and reporting all want the same event and none of them care about the others. Using one primitive for both would either over-engineer the fan-out or lose the ordering guarantee the cascade depends on.
  • Rate limits as a ledger, not a retry loop. Every venue gets a token bucket sized to its published limits, and callers take a token before the request rather than discovering the limit by being refused. When a venue does return a rate-limit response, its headers are parsed and honoured rather than backed off by guess. The reason this matters for throughput: uncoordinated concurrency against a rate-limited API doesn't fail cleanly, it degrades: you get slower and throttled at once. Metering at the source keeps the pipe full without tripping it.
  • Every venue error classified, once. Each (venue, error_code) pair carries a declared classification: whether the call is safe to retry, whether the connection needs re-opening, and which of four actions to take: retry, reconnect, skip, or fail. Handling is therefore uniform across venues, and adding a venue means declaring its error codes rather than teaching the pipeline a new dialect. Guessing from a message string is how a permanent failure becomes an infinite retry.

Guarding against the venue changing underneath us

One more, because it is the failure mode most likely to bite a live carry book quietly. Venue APIs change: a field is renamed, a type loosens, an error code appears. To catch that in CI rather than in production, real venue responses are recorded as fixtures and replayed against our parsers on every change. A venue altering its response shape breaks a test in a pull request; it does not surface as a mis-parsed funding rate on a Tuesday. Where a venue's protocol cannot be recorded that way, the client is mocked at its own interface instead, deliberately, and that exception is written down rather than assumed.

One cost note, since it affects your retainer indirectly: heavy work never runs on a laptop. Full-corpus scans, manifest rewrites and bulk backfills run on in-region VMs, and backfill VMs default to spot instances with preemption recovery that resumes from measured progress rather than replaying from the start date. We also audit the fleet for two specific waste patterns: a preempted VM with no matching recovery, and a machine sized far larger than its measured usage, because neither shows up in a bill as anything other than a slightly larger number.

The treasury is keyed by share class (the fund's base currency), not by chain. Clients deposit into the treasury for their share class; which chain that wallet lives on is an implementation detail underneath. When the treasury falls below its floor, strategies reduce positions and capital flows back up from the hot wallets. When it rises above its ceiling (a deposit lands, say) the excess flows down and strategies scale in. Where the target wallet is on another chain, the strategy initiates the bridge itself as part of the same flow.

The mechanism detail that matters: a rebalance is emitted as a strategy instruction, not as an administrative side-channel. The strategy sees the deposit event, evaluates the ratio, and emits transfer instructions through the same path as a trade. So every capital movement lands in the same ledger, carries the same attribution, and is reproduced identically in a batch rerun: a rebalance cannot silently break the determinism proof in §5, because it is not outside the system doing the proving. On the CeFi side the shape is simpler: no treasury-and-hot split, one wallet per venue.

And the boundary that never moves: all of this operates within a single client's hierarchy. Balancing happens between that client's treasury and that client's strategy wallets. It never crosses a client boundary, and as §1 sets out, the code will not represent a transfer that would.

Risk, and what happens when a venue misbehaves

Kill-switches operate at wallet level, and the direction is asymmetric by design: arming a protective stop is always automatic, while resuming trading is only automatic within a defined recovery matrix and otherwise requires a human. Venue circuit breakers trip on classified error conditions and fail over rather than retrying blindly, and per-shard failures are isolated so one venue's outage does not take down the run. Exchange freezes and withdrawal suspensions have defined automated responses.

Seventeen worst-case scenarios (drawn from real CeFi and DeFi failures rather than invented) are injected directly into the production code path so we can verify that the correct breaker, kill-switch and alert actually fire: exchange failures, oracle problems, stablecoin de-pegs, liquidation cascades, gas spikes, venue outages. Writing those tests surfaced several real weaknesses, which is exactly what they were for.

The attribution feed your fund administrator consumes

Your fund administration stays yours; what the strategy hands it is a clean input rather than raw fills. P&L is attributed rather than merely totalled: every row carries a factor, delta, funding, basis, carry, fees, slippage, a layer separating strategy decisions from execution quality, and a venue and instrument. The strategy reads its own attribution back and adapts to it. High-water marks are computed from the trade ledger by three simultaneous methods (time-weighted return, notional, and P&L recovery) and never from a running maximum of raw equity, which is the standard way a high-water mark quietly misstates itself after a capital flow.

What that means in practice, without overstating it

Two honest halves. The first is that a new market, a new venue or a new strategy family arrives as an integration against established contracts rather than as a rebuild, which is why the second venue costs a fraction of the first, and the fifth costs a fraction of the second. The second half is that "a fraction" is not "nothing": every one of them involves real work, and the work that does not compress is the part that touches the outside world. Live-probing a venue's actual collateral behaviour, calibrating against it, and running a careful cutover take the time they take regardless of how good the abstraction underneath is.

The distinction worth carrying away is that the expensive parts of building a trading platform are mostly one-time and mostly already paid. The parts that recur per market are the parts that were always going to be specific: what the instrument is, what is dangerous about it, and how a return should be decomposed.
